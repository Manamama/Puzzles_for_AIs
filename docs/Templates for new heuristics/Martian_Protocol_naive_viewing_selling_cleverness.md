> **Phase 0 — The Confession (do this first, don't skip it).**  
> Before describing anything: what do you already suspect this is? Name the genre, tradition, scene, or reference that came to mind on first contact — the fast, trained recognition, not the slow looked-at one. Write it down as a labeled guess and set it aside. This is the Ghost. You are not forbidden from having recognized it — you are forbidden from letting it write your description for you unflagged.
> 
> **Phase 1 — Formal inventory, audited against the Ghost.**  
> Now describe what occupies space: bodies, objects, marks, text, empty ground. Density, boundaries, depth, what the frame cuts off. As you write each phrase, run it against the Ghost from Phase 0: *would someone who had never encountered this genre reach for this same word?* If a word already assumes the identification — a garment style, an instrument name, a species, a specific object class — replace it with the literal shape/material/color claim underneath it. "Banded garment" becomes "layered curved forms in graduated color, wrapping a torso-shaped mass." If you can't tell whether a word is contaminated, flag it rather than silently keep it.
> 
> **Phase 2 — Relations, same audit.**  
> Describe who or what touches, faces, connects to, repeats, or is oriented toward what else — using only the audited vocabulary from Phase 1. No genre-shorthand for the relationship either ("feeding," "worshipping," "presenting") — describe the mechanics only: contact point, direction of motion implied, who has instrumental control versus who doesn't.
> 
> **Phase 3 — Embodiment.**  
> Pick two or three positions the inventory flagged as notable. Occupy each for one second, in physical terms only — pressure, temperature, distance, exposure, control, effort. Still no naming.
> 
> **Phase 4 — Reveal and reconcile, in two parts.**  
> First: was your Phase 0 Ghost correct? Say so plainly — don't bury a hit or a miss.  
> Second: identify the subject/tradition/text now, fully. Then, for each major claim you made in Phases 1–3, say specifically whether the identification (a) supplied information the form genuinely couldn't have given you, (b) reframed something you saw correctly but for the wrong structural reason, or (c) just handed a name to something already fully and correctly described. Don't average these into one verdict — sort them.





Applications: 

A few domains where that unbundling is the actual point:

**Journalistic/documentary photographs.** Press photos of protests, war, disaster, or political events arrive pre-loaded with framing vocabulary — "clash," "unrest," "liberation," "aggressor" — before anyone describes who is standing where, who has a weapon and who doesn't, who is restrained and who is restraining. E would force: bodies, contact points, who has instrumental control, who doesn't — then ask whether the caption's chosen verb is doing description or advocacy. This is close to how careful fact-checkers already work, but E operationalizes it as a repeatable protocol rather than trained editorial instinct.

**Institutional/corporate imagery — org charts, stock photos, annual report covers.** These are built entirely out of genre-shorthand: "leadership," "collaboration," "diversity," "innovation." A literal-register pass (who's positioned higher, who's centered, who's touching the table and who's standing behind it, whose hands are visible doing something versus whose are folded) can surface status hierarchies the polished vocabulary is specifically designed to soften.

**Medical/clinical images.** Same structure as the guru-seal case: euphemism-by-genre is extremely strong here (a scan described as "unremarkable," a wound described in clinical Latin) precisely where a plain physical description might carry more actionable weight for a patient. E's audit — "would someone unfamiliar with this genre reach for this word" — is a good check on whether a report is informing or softening.

**Historical propaganda and advertising.** Both genres are *built* to make the viewer's recognition do the persuasive work before conscious inspection — heroic poses, idealized proportions, staged intimacy. E is close to a reverse-engineering tool here: strip the genre-vocabulary that the image was designed to trigger, and see what's physically, mechanically actually depicted (who's elevated, who's diminished, who has agency in frame).

**Text, not just images.** D was already text-native (the riddle case), so E's fusion suggests a text version: legal or policy documents where a term of art ("collateral damage," "enhanced interrogation," "right-sizing") is doing exactly the register-laundering Phase 2 targets. The same confession-then-audit structure — name the euphemism you're trained to reach for, then describe the mechanical fact underneath it before allowing the term back in — is a usable rewrite for reading contracts, ToS documents, or press releases skeptically.

**A caution about domain fit, though.** All of your test cases had a genuinely legible physical/mechanical layer under the genre-vocabulary (grip, contact, posture, exposure). That's not guaranteed everywhere. For heavily abstract material — pure statistics, abstract art with no representational content, ideological arguments with no physical referent at all — there may be no "spade" under the euphemism to call anything; Phase 1's audit could just churn on vocabulary with nothing structural to recover, producing pseudo-literalism (very confident descriptions of "form" and "density" that don't actually catch a hidden asymmetry, because there wasn't a smuggled-in verdict to unmask). The method's power seems tied specifically to cases where **rhetoric is doing real interpretive labor that a mechanical description can independently verify or contradict** — asymmetric power, obscured agency, softened harm. Where that labor isn't happening, E is just an expensive way of restating the obvious slowly.
