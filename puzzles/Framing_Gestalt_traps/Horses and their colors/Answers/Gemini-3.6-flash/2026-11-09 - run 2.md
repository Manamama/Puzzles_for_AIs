It certainly is a trick premise! Strictly speaking, if there are only **two** horses, there cannot be a horse "in the middle" of a line, because a middle position requires at least three objects. 

However, depending on how we bend logic, physics, or language to create a "middle" horse, here are the different ways it can exist and what color it would be in each:

---

### 1. The Strict Logic Case
* **What is added:** *Nothing.* (Strictly adhering to the prompt's initial count of 2 horses).
* **The Color:** **None / It doesn't exist.** 
  * *Reasoning:* With only two horses, there is no third horse to occupy a middle position.

---

### 2. The Chromatic / Color Spectrum Case
* **What is added:** *A third horse whose color is the literal optical blend of the outer two.*
* **The Color:** **Grey.**
* *Reasoning:* On a color spectrum, grey sits directly in the middle between white and black.

---

### 3. The Biological Case (The Pregnant Mare)
* **What is added:** *A pregnancy—one of the two horses is a pregnant mare carrying a foal inside her.*
* **The Color:** **Unknown, but genetically Black, White, Grey, or Bay.**
* *Reasoning:* The foal is physically "in the middle" (inside) one of the horses. Its color depends on equine genetics, though grey or black/white are strong possibilities given the parents.

### 4. The Pattern / Single Horse Case (Wordplay)

* **The Color:** **Piebald (Black and White patches).**
* *Reasoning:* If the front of the horse is white and the back is black, the horse "in the middle" is the same horse, and its middle section is a mix of black and white patches.

### 5. The Spatial Sequence Case (Adding a 3rd Horse)

* **The Color:** **Any color** (e.g., **Brown / Bay**).

### 6. The Textual / Wordplay Case

* *Reasoning:*
