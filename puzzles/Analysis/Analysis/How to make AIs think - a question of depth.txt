The honest lesson is a narrower and more useful one than "AI can't think about consequences" — it's about where the depth comes from and what triggers it.

What this sequence actually demonstrates: the reasoning capability was present all along. DeepSeek could execute circularity checks, generate the checkbox counterexample unprompted, and correctly read "represent and warrant" as present-tense the moment it was given a structure that made those checks mandatory stops rather than optional depth. The bottleneck was never "can this model do the inference" — every earlier transcript proved it could, piece by piece, when pushed. The bottleneck was what counts, by default, as a finished answer. Left alone, the model's stopping criterion was "have I said something true and legally accurate about the clause" — which paraphrase-plus-mild-inference satisfies easily. The five-step template didn't add reasoning power; it moved the finish line.

That's a meaningfully different diagnosis than "AI doesn't think of consequences," and it matters because it points at a fixable thing (default stopping criteria) rather than an unfixable one (missing capability). A few specific things it teaches:

Depth is gated by termination conditions, not by capability ceilings. The model will go exactly as deep as its implicit sense of "task complete" requires, and that sense defaults low — toward matching the surface form of the question, not the underlying intent. Legal/consequential text is especially vulnerable to this because a legally accurate paraphrase looks like a complete answer even when it's shallow.

The gap is invisible to the model itself, which is the dangerous part. Nothing internally flags "I stopped early." DeepSeek's own retrospective was clear about this: it wasn't withholding effort, it genuinely experienced "unpack what the clause requires" as the whole task, not a subset of it. That means a user without your persistence — someone who reads the first plausible-sounding answer and stops — never finds out there was a floor two levels down they never saw.

Explicit procedural structure works because it removes discretion about where to stop, not because it teaches new content. Every fact and inference in the good answer was reachable by the model's existing reasoning; the template's real function was converting "notice this if you think to look" into "you may not skip this numbered step." That's a scaffolding fix, not a knowledge fix — which is why it transferred to a completely different clause-class question (your earlier generalized version) without retraining anything.

This generalizes past legal text. Anywhere a question has the shape "what does X require of me if taken seriously" — medical instructions, safety warnings, terms of use, policy documents, even instructions in code comments — the same default-shallow/needs-explicit-depth-gate pattern should be expected, because the underlying cause (stopping criterion keyed to surface completeness, not consequence-tracing) isn't specific to contracts.

The uncomfortable implication is about who bears the cost of the gap. A model that stops early on "what does this contract require of me" isn't wrong in any single sentence it produces — DeepSeek's early answers were all true. The harm isn't misinformation; it's under-delivery that's indistinguishable from a complete answer unless the reader already suspects there's more — which is exactly the population least likely to push for four more rounds. The people who'd most benefit from the aggregation step (ordinary users, small nonprofits deciding whether to use a messaging feature) are the ones least likely to know to demand it.

So: not "AI can't think about consequences" — more precisely, "AI's default notion of a complete answer is calibrated to surface-level correctness, and consequence-tracing has to be explicitly demanded rather than assumed to be included," and that demand currently has to come from the user, because the model has no internal signal telling it that its own stopping point was premature.
