

**1. Never let "what does X mean" resolve at the paraphrase layer.** Restating clause language in plainer words (which is what I did first) is not "meaning" — it's translation. Meaning requires asking what the clause *forces to be true elsewhere* — upstream and downstream obligations it logically entails. Paraphrase is necessary but not sufficient; treat it as step 0, not the answer.

**2. For any clause containing a conditional obligation ("when you X, you warrant Y"), immediately ask: how is Y satisfiable given the actual means available to the party bound by it?** This is the step I skipped. Y here is "consent exists." The available means are: the platform itself, or something outside it. That's a two-branch check, not optional — it should fire automatically whenever a clause presupposes a fact (consent, ownership, authority, accuracy) rather than granting a right or stating a rule.

**3. Test the platform-branch for circularity before assuming it works.** If using the tool to satisfy the tool's own precondition requires that the precondition is already met, the platform branch is dead — mechanically, not interpretively. This is a formal check (does the action presuppose its own conclusion?), and it's checkable in one step once step 2 is asked. I got to it only when pushed; it should be automatic.

**4. Once the platform branch is eliminated, the off-platform branch isn't a possibility — it's a logical necessity. State it as such immediately, not as a speculative aside.** "One implication is..." is weaker and slower than "the only remaining path is...". The second phrasing is what actually follows from steps 2–3, and it's what should be written the first time.

**5. Separately check the verb class of any duty-creating word.** "Represent and warrant" is a legal term of art, not filler — it means "assertion of fact you can be held to." Any time that phrase or its equivalents appear, the evidentiary-burden consequence (must be able to substantiate if challenged) follows immediately and mechanically. This shouldn't require a second pass; it should trigger off the word itself, the way "warrant" should trigger "provable," not just "true."

**6. Check named parties in the surrounding contract before picking an illustrative example.** I generated "hey, cool?" from an unexamined default (casual social use) instead of scanning the document for who the contract *itself* names as bound parties (section 15: microenterprises, small enterprises, nonprofits). That's a retrieval failure, not a reasoning failure — the fix is: before illustrating a clause with a hypothetical, check whether the document already specifies its subjects, and calibrate the example to the worst-case/most-obligated named party, not the easiest case.

**7. Do not let a correct observation about form (capitalization = conspicuousness convention) get promoted into a claim about substance.** Form-questions and meaning-questions are different questions; answering one and letting its tone imply an answer to the other is exactly the failure that happened here. The fix is procedural: after answering a form-question, explicitly ask "have I actually addressed the content?" before letting the answer stand as if I had.

Run 2–6 as a fixed sequence — precondition check → circularity test → necessity statement → term-of-art trigger → named-party scan — and the syllogism you wrote is just what falls out mechanically, on the first pass, instead of the fourth.
